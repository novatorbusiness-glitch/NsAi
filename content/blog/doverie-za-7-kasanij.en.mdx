No one buys from a stranger. Even a flawless offer that removes every objection breaks against a wall of distrust if the person is seeing you for the first time in their life. Trust doesn't appear at the click of a button — it's built up piece by piece, touch by touch. The only question is whether you build it deliberately or hope it shows up on its own.

## Why one touch doesn't work

A client on social media lives in a digital trance. They scroll the feed on autopilot, the brain runs in economy mode and filters almost everything out as noise. One post, one ad, one email — that's too weak a signal to pull someone out of that state.

First contact isn't a sale — it's an introduction. It earns you the right to ask the right question, not to show off a storefront. From there, you have to walk a path: from "who are you?" to "I need what you do." And that path is laid down by a sequence of touches.

## The four elements of trust

Any trust is built from four blocks, and a touch sequence lays them out step by step — one per contact, not crammed into a single post:

- **Expertise** — the answer to "why should I listen to you specifically?" Case studies with numbers, a breakdown of your methodology, live examples.
- **The problem** — "why does this matter to me right now?" You bring the pain into focus and show the cost of inaction.
- **Transformation** — "what result will I get?" You project the person into the future they want: what they look like once the problem is solved.
- **The product** — "how do I get that result?" Only at the end do you openly talk about the solution.

Trying to fit all four into one piece of content is like telling the whole story of how you met on a first date. The brain can't keep up, and the filter slams shut. That's why each block gets its own touch.

## What a 7-touch sequence looks like

Take a simple scenario for an expert selling consultations:

1. **Touch 1.** A useful post about a mistake your audience commonly makes. The reader recognizes their own pain.
2. **Touch 2.** A lead magnet: a checklist or mini-guide that solves one narrow problem. The person leaves their contact — a first act of goodwill.
3. **Touch 3.** An email with a case study: a specific result with numbers. This is expertise.
4. **Touch 4.** A breakdown email: why the old ways don't work anymore, and what inaction costs. This is pain.
5. **Touch 5.** A client story: from "everything was falling apart" to the result. This is transformation.
6. **Touch 6.** An announcement email: how the work is structured, what's included, who it isn't for. This is the product, and it defuses fears.
7. **Touch 7.** An offer with a clear reason to act now: spots, a deadline, a guarantee.

Each touch isn't "one more sales pitch" — it's another brick. By step seven, the person is already mentally "living" alongside you: they've gotten value, seen proof, recognized themselves in the stories. The offer to buy no longer feels like a leap into the unknown — it feels like the logical next step.

## The mistake that kills the sequence

The sequence breaks when the touches don't connect to each other: the post is written in one voice, the email in another, and the offer sells an entirely different topic. The brain catches the mismatch and decides: "something's off." Predictive processing glitches — and the person leaves.

So the sequence is built as a single route: one theme, one voice, one sequence of meaning. Tone, visuals and logic don't shift from step to step.

## How to automate this

Writing seven touches by hand for every client is impossible — and unnecessary. The sequence is set up once: a lead magnet captures the contact, emails and bot messages go out on schedule, and the system decides on its own what to show whom next. Trust gets built without you personally being in every conversation.
